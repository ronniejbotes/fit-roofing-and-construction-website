Do you need a permit to replace a roof in Plano?

Yes. The City of Plano lists replacing a roof among the jobs that need a building permit, though a small repair such as replacing a few shingles generally doesn't need one. The permit is normally taken out by a contractor registered with the city, and the job ends with a final inspection that includes the attic. For your own house, the city's Building Inspections department has the final word.

Plano treats a new roof as building work. If you're planning a roof replacement in Plano, get the permit sorted before work starts.

The rules below come from the city's own handouts and its residential code. It's general information, and only the city can rule on your house.

When does a roof job in Plano need a permit?

Whenever you're replacing the roof. The city's handout When is a Permit Required? puts replacing a roof on its list of projects that need a permit, next to foundation repairs, water heaters and fences. The form the city uses for the final roof inspection is blunter. It says a roof permit is required on all re-roof projects.

The code says the same thing by leaving roofing out. Plano adopted the 2024 International Residential Code, with its own amendments, in 2025. Its list of building work that's exempt from a permit covers things like small detached sheds, low retaining walls, playground equipment, and finish work such as painting and cabinets. Roofing isn't on it.

The reason for the new roof doesn't change any of this. A worn-out roof and a storm-damaged one both need a permit to be replaced.

Can you repair part of a roof without a permit?

Yes, if the job is small. The city's re-roof permit handout says re-roof work not exceeding 25% of the roof doesn't require a permit. Replacing a few shingles, resealing flashing or fitting a new vent boot comes in far below that line.

Two cautions go with it. The handout carries a 2016 revision date and was written for the 2015 code. The residential code Plano adopted in 2025 doesn't repeat the 25% figure, and it exempts ordinary repairs from a permit without putting a size on them. The city also says its handouts are for information only and shouldn't be relied on in place of its regulations. If your job is anywhere near the line, or you're not sure how the city would measure it, ask Building Inspections before work starts.

If a stain on a ceiling is what got you thinking about the roof, work out where the water is coming from first. Whether to patch the roof or replace it is a separate decision, covered in roof repair vs replacement.

Who pulls the roofing permit, you or the roofer?

Usually the roofer. Plano's residential code says a permit applicant has to be registered with the city as a contractor. Homeowners are the exception, because they can get a permit for work at their own residence without registering. The re-roof handout says the same for a homeowner doing the work on their own homestead.

The city's advice on this is pointed. The second page of its When is a Permit Required? handout carries tips on choosing a contractor. They say never to use one who claims no permits or inspections are required, and to be especially wary of a contractor who suggests you get the permit for their work. If you take out the permit for a contractor, the city says, you're responsible for their work.

So if you hire a roofer, the roofer should apply under its own registration. The same tips tell you to insist on seeing the permit before the project begins and to keep an eye on the contractor's inspection record as the work goes on. You can check both yourself. The city's online permit system, eTRAKiT, reached from its Building Inspections page, lets you search permits and registered contractors.

The code also gives a roofer a reason to see the inspections through. A contractor's registration can be suspended for failing to finalize permits by getting the required inspections approved. As for cost, the re-roof handout gives no amount and points to the city's fee schedule instead.

What does the city check when a new roof goes on?

The re-roof handout lists rules for the materials. Like its 25% line, they were written for the 2015 code, so check anything unusual about your roof with Building Inspections. In plain terms, the handout says:

  • An existing roof of wood shake, slate, clay or cement tile, or asbestos-cement tile has to come off before the new roof goes on, with some exceptions set out in the code.
  • Unpackaged shingles, which the handout calls seconds, can't be used.
  • Rotten decking or rafters have to be replaced as needed.

If your house still has a wood shake roof, raise it with your roofer early. Whether the shakes can stay under a new roof depends on what goes on top and what state they're in, and the answer changes the whole job.

For metal and tile roofs, the handout also lists a partial inspection of the underlayment, before the roof covering goes on. Every re-roof then gets a final inspection to confirm that the materials were installed properly and meet the code.

The final inspection reaches into the attic. According to the city's form, the inspector checks the vent piping that runs through the roof, that the rafters are in position, and that the water heater and furnace exhaust vents were put back properly. Roofers work around those vents during a tear-off, so it's a sensible thing to check.

Can you skip the city's final roof inspection?

Yes, by signing a form, but read it closely first. The city's Certification of Final Roof Installation says the final roof inspection comes as part of your roof permit. If you choose to waive it, you, the homeowner, sign a statement that your roofing contractor has done the same attic check. The form then goes back to the city by fax or email.

You'd be putting your name to a statement about work you probably didn't watch, in an attic you may never go into. The city's inspection already comes with the permit.

Does your HOA need to approve a new roof too?

It might. Both of the city's handouts tell homeowners to check with their homeowners association for extra requirements in the deed restrictions and covenants. That's a separate check from the city permit, so ask the HOA what it needs to see before you sign a contract.

Where should you start if you're replacing a roof in Plano?

Read the re-roof handout before you sign a contract. It's posted on the city's Residential Additions & Alterations page, along with the final inspection form. Then check your HOA's rules. When you hire, have the roofer pull the permit under its own registration and show it to you before the old roof comes off.

Is a water stain on your ceiling a roof leak, and what should you do next?

A water stain on your ceiling is often a roof leak, but plumbing and air conditioning cause plenty of them too. Timing is the best first clue. A stain that spreads after rain points to the roof, one that grows in dry weather points to a pipe, and one that appears on hot days while the AC runs points to its drain. Find the source before you paint, because paint won't stop the water.

Brown rings on a ceiling get blamed on the roof almost by default. Sometimes the roof is guilty. Often it isn't, and no roofer can fix a leaking shower drain.

The stain itself can tell you a lot if you watch when it changes and note where it sits. The attic usually tells you the rest.

How can you tell if a ceiling stain is from the roof?

Watch when it changes. The day you notice the stain, draw a light pencil line around its edge and date it. Check it after the next rain, and again after a dry week.

A stain that grows after rain but not in dry spells means water is getting in from outside, so the roof is the first suspect. Growth on dry days points indoors: to a pipe, or to the AC if it happens on hot, sticky days while the AC runs hard.

Position matters too. Roof leaks show up on top-floor ceilings, often near an outside wall, a chimney, a skylight or a plumbing vent pipe. Water also travels before it drips. It can run along the roof deck or down a rafter and land well away from the hole. Start looking directly above the stain, then work uphill along the rafters.

In a two-story house, think about which room sits over the stain. A stain on the dining room ceiling, right under an upstairs bathroom, is a plumbing question first. Rain would have to get past the attic and the second floor and run down inside a wall to land there. It happens, but a worn toilet seal, a shower pan or a supply line is the likelier culprit.

What else causes a brown stain on the ceiling?

A brown stain only tells you that water soaked in and dried. The EPA's list of common moisture problems in buildings includes leaking roofs, leaking or condensing water pipes, and gutters that send water into a building. It also points to poorly draining air handler drain pans as a hiding place for moisture (EPA mold course).

The AC deserves a hard look in Dallas and the rest of North Texas, where it gets little rest in summer. As it cools, it pulls water out of the air, and that water drains from a pan under the indoor unit through a condensate line. If the air handler sits in your attic and the line clogs or the pan rusts through, the water lands in the ceiling. The EPA advises keeping AC drip pans clean and drain lines flowing (EPA guide to mold and moisture).

Ducts can drip too. A cold duct running through a hot attic can sweat if its insulation is torn or missing. The EPA says ducts in spaces that aren't air conditioned should be sealed and insulated to prevent condensation. A bathroom fan that blows into the attic adds moisture up there. The Department of Energy's Building America Solution Center says those fans should vent outdoors, not into the attic (Building America guidance).

What should you look for in the attic?

Look for water where it shouldn't be: wet insulation, a damp roof deck or a full AC drain pan. If you can get up there safely, the attic usually settles the question. Go in the cool of the morning with a good flashlight, and step only on joists or a walkway. A foot through the drywall turns one ceiling repair into two.

To find the spot above the stain, measure how far it sits from an outside wall in the room below, then measure the same distance in the attic. Check for:

  • wet or matted insulation
  • dark streaks or soft patches on the underside of the roof deck, especially around the chimney, vent pipes and skylights
  • daylight around a pipe or where the roof meets a wall
  • nail tips with rust or water drops on them, a sign of moisture from a leak or from condensation
  • water in the AC drain pan, a rusted pan, or a drain line that has come apart
  • damp duct insulation, or a bathroom fan duct that ends in the attic

After a rain, a wet roof deck right above the stain is about as clear an answer as you'll get. A dry deck above a full drain pan or a sweating duct points away from the roof. Take photos either way. If you can't get up there safely, leave it to a roofer or an HVAC technician.

Does a ceiling stain after rain always mean a roof leak?

Not always. Gutters and upstairs windows let rain in too, and when the roof is to blame, the shingles often aren't.

Flashing is the usual suspect. It's the metal that seals the joints where the roof meets a wall or a chimney, and around skylights. When it lifts or its sealant cracks, water runs in behind it. The rubber boot around a plumbing vent pipe is another weak point, because sun and heat dry it out until it splits. Both are roof repairs, even when the shingles look fine.

Gutters can do it too. When they clog or pull away from the fascia, water spills behind them into the top of the wall and leaves a stain along the edge of the ceiling. The EPA lists cleaning and repairing gutters as a moisture-control step. If yours overflow or are pulling loose, the fix is in the gutters rather than the roof, and if they need replacing, that's gutter installation work.

Upstairs windows are the other common route. Wind-driven rain gets behind trim that has lost its caulk and runs down inside the wall. If the stain only shows after storms from one direction, look hard at that side of the house. If it appeared right after hail, have the roof checked for hail damage as well.

What should you do about the stain today?

Photograph it with something in the frame for scale, and keep the dated pencil lines going. If it's dripping, put a bucket under it and move furniture and electronics away. If water is pouring in during a storm right now, start with what to do while your roof is leaking.

Some signs mean it can't wait:

  • The ceiling sags, bulges or feels soft. Keep people and pets out from under it.
  • Water is near a light fixture, fan or smoke alarm. Switch that circuit off at the breaker until the area is dry and an electrician has checked it.
  • There's a musty smell, or dark spots are spreading.

Don't wait for the next storm to prove it's the roof, either. A slow leak soaks the same insulation and drywall every time it rains. If you're tempted to leave it, read what a small roof leak costs if you wait.

When can you paint over a water stain on the ceiling?

Only once the source is fixed and the ceiling is fully dry. Paint over a damp patch and the stain comes back.

The EPA is plain on this. Dry wet materials quickly, and don't paint over mold, because paint on moldy surfaces is likely to peel. Its guide also explains which mold you can clean up yourself and when to call a professional.

Drywall that has gone soft or saggy needs cutting out and replacing, and wet insulation above it should be dried or replaced. On sound, dry drywall, painters seal the stain with a stain-blocking primer first. Ceiling paint on its own tends to let the stain bleed back through.

What's the next step if you've found a stain?

  1. Mark and photograph the stain, then watch it through the next rain and the next dry spell.
  2. Check the attic above it, if you can do that safely.
  3. Call the trade that fits what you found: a roofer for a wet deck or failed flashing (see roof leak repair), a plumber for a stain under a bathroom, an HVAC technician for a full drain pan or sweating ducts.
  4. Paint only after the cause is fixed and the ceiling has dried out.

Roof leaking during a storm? What to do before the roofer gets there

If your roof is leaking during a storm, the first thing to do is deal with the dangers: keep away from water near outlets, light fixtures and anything plugged in, and keep everyone out from under a sagging ceiling. Cut the power to the wet area at the breaker only if you can reach the panel standing on a dry floor. Then catch the water, move your things and take photos. Stay off the roof and off outdoor ladders until the storm has passed, because the roof and any tarp can wait.

This guide runs in the order that matters tonight: the power, the ceiling, the water, your records and then the call to a roofer. Whether a slow drip can wait is a separate question, covered in our post on the cost of leaving a small leak.

Should you turn off the power when water comes through the ceiling?

Yes, if water is reaching anything electrical and you can do it safely. Water near wiring is what makes a roof leak an emergency. Ready.gov's flood advice puts it plainly: "Do not touch electrical equipment if it is wet or if you are standing in water." It also says to turn the electricity off if it is safe to do so.

In practice, that means switching off the breaker for the wet room, or the main, while you stand on a dry floor with dry hands. If the panel is in a wet garage, or you would have to walk through water to reach it, leave it alone. Call your electric utility or an electrician, and keep everyone out of that part of the house. If you see sparks or smell burning, get everyone outside and call 911.

Water dripping from a light fixture or a ceiling fan is the clearest case for cutting the power. So is water running down a wall that has outlets in it.

Thunder adds one more rule. The National Weather Service says that indoors during a thunderstorm you should not touch anything plugged into an electrical outlet. If something plugged in is getting wet, switch off its circuit at the panel rather than pulling the plug. The wet-vac and the fans can wait until the thunder has moved off.

What do you do when the ceiling is bulging with water?

Get everyone out from under it, pets included. FEMA's guide to repairing a flooded home warns that a lot of wind and rain can leave a ceiling holding water, and that wet plaster or wallboard is very heavy and dangerous if it falls.

Treat any room where a tree or a big limb has come through the roof the same way, and keep that door shut.

Do not try to drain the ceiling or pull it down yourself, and do not step under it to set out a bucket. Keep the room empty and lay towels along the threshold to hold back the water.

FEMA's guide notes that a sagging ceiling has to be replaced anyway, so there is nothing to save by getting close to it. Leave it to a professional once the storm has passed.

Can you stop a roof leak from inside the house?

Not really. The opening is on the roof, and only a roof repair from outside closes it. From inside, you can control where the water goes and how far it spreads.

Put a bucket or tub under each drip, with an old towel in the bottom to stop the splashing. If a drip is coming from a sagging patch, leave it and keep clear. Move furniture and rugs out of the way. Cover what you cannot move with plastic sheeting or a shower curtain, and lay towels where water is creeping across the floor.

If you have attic access and the ladder or stairs up to it are safe to use, take a flashlight up. Step only on the joists or on boards laid across them, since the drywall between them will not hold you, and keep your hands off wiring and light fixture boxes. Set a bucket on a scrap of plywood across the joists, never straight on the drywall. Pull back insulation that is soaked so it stops holding water against the ceiling. Then photograph the spot where water is coming through the roof deck.

Expect the drip and the hole to be in different places. Water often runs along a rafter or the underside of the deck before it falls. The stain on your ceiling can sit some way from the real opening, which is why those attic photos matter.

Why does a roof leak only in heavy rain or wind?

Because ordinary rain runs down a roof the way it was built to, and storm rain does not. Wind drives water sideways, under shingle edges and around flashing. A heavy downpour can overwhelm a valley where two slopes meet, or the flashing behind a chimney.

That is how a roof stays dry through a whole season and then leaks in one storm. The usual places to look are the flashing around chimneys, vents and skylights, lifted or missing shingles, and valleys.

Is it safe to go on the roof or tarp it during the storm?

No. The National Weather Service is blunt that there is no safe place outside when thunderstorms are in the area. On a wet slope in gusting wind, one slip off a ladder can do more harm than the leak ever will.

A tarp is the right short-term fix. It goes on after the storm has passed, in daylight, by someone with the gear and the footing to work on a roof. Covering a damaged roof this way is a normal part of storm repair.

What should you photograph and keep for your records?

Photograph the damage before you clean anything up. Take wide shots of each wet room and close-ups of the ceiling, the drip, the attic and anything that got soaked. A short video shows water moving in a way a still photo cannot. Write down when the leak started.

The Texas Department of Insurance's tips for storm damage add two things worth doing now. Make a list of the damage inside and outside. Save the receipts for what you spend to stop more damage, such as tarps, buckets and fans.

For anything about coverage, check your policy and talk to your insurer.

What should you tell the roofer when you call?

Tell them where the water shows up inside, when it started and what the storm brought, whether that was wind, hail or a falling limb. Mention whether it leaks in every rain or only in wind, any past repairs in that spot, and anything still open to the weather. If you know the age and material of the roof, say so. Have your photos ready to send.

If a storm line comes through Arlington overnight and water starts dripping from the hallway light, your first move is the breaker, and the roofer comes second. When you do call, "water through the hallway light, a bathroom vent on the roof above it, started around midnight with strong wind from the south" tells a roofer far more than "my roof is leaking."

What comes next once the rain stops?

Start drying. The EPA's guidance on indoor leaks is to act quickly, since wet materials that are dried promptly usually do not grow mold. Once the thunder has gone and the power in that area is safe to use, run fans and a dehumidifier and wet-vac the carpet. Leave any circuit with a wet light fixture or outlet switched off until an electrician has checked it. Keep the attic insulation pulled back until the drywall below has dried.

Then get the roof looked at, even if the dripping has stopped. A leak that stops with the rain has not fixed itself.

If the roofer says the roof needs replacing rather than repairing, ask for the reasons in writing. Compare them with what roof repair versus replacement usually comes down to. If it does come to a new roof, that is also the time to decide whether to replace your gutters at the same time.

Should you replace your gutters when you replace your roof?

Replace your gutters when you replace your roof if they are dented, sagging, leaking at the joints, spilling over in a hard rain even when clean, or hanging from soft, rotten fascia. If they are straight, drain to the downspouts and are fixed to solid wood, you can keep them. The roofer then has to protect them during the tear-off and leave them sitting under the new drip edge. Make the call before the old shingles come off, because the roof edge and the gutters get worked on together.

A roof and its gutters meet along one narrow strip at the eaves, and a roof replacement rebuilds that strip from the deck up. So the gutters belong in the roofing plan from the start. Below is how the two fit together, what a tear-off does to old gutters, which ones are worth keeping and the order the work should go in.

This post sticks to the re-roof decision. For the bigger picture on what gutters do for a house, see how Texas gutters protect your home from water damage.

How are the gutters and the roof edge connected?

They share the eave. Along the bottom edge of a shingle roof runs a strip of bent metal called drip edge. It throws water clear of the edge, which helps keep it from curling back onto the fascia board and the wood deck and rotting them. The Asphalt Roofing Manufacturers Association strongly recommends drip edge on all shingle roofs and points out that local codes may require it.

At the eave, the drip edge normally goes on before the underlayment. That makes it part of building the new roof, not a piece of trim added at the end.

The gutter hangs just below it. Gutter makers' installation instructions have you slip the back of the gutter under the drip edge, so water coming off the roof drops into the gutter instead of running down behind it. When the roof edge is rebuilt, the gutter is right there in the way.

What happens to old gutters during a tear-off?

The crew either takes them down first or works around them. Either way, they take some punishment.

Gutters are thin metal. Ladders lean on them, and the old shingles, nails and underlayment all slide over them on the way down. A run that was already tired can come out of that bent or pulled loose.

Some gutters do not hang from the fascia at all. They hang from straps nailed to the roof deck and tucked under the first row of shingles. A tear-off disturbs every one of those straps.

Putting gutters back is harder than it sounds. Each run is hung with a slight fall toward its downspout, and one rehung a little off holds water in the low spots or spills at the far end. Once the gutters are down, you also see the fascia for the first time in years. If it is soft, nothing should go back on it until it is replaced.

Which gutters should you replace with the roof, and which can stay?

Judge them on the metal, the drainage, what holds them up and the wood behind them, and on color if the look of the house matters to you. This is how the signs usually split.

Spilling over needs a closer look before you blame the gutter. A gutter that overflows may be clogged, pitched wrong, short of downspouts or too small for the roof above it. Cleaning fixes the first problem. The rest need new work, and a re-roof is the sensible time to do it.

What if hail is the reason for the new roof?

Then look hard at the gutters before anyone decides. Hail that bruises shingles dents soft metal too, including gutters, downspouts and vent caps. After a spring hailstorm in Plano, for instance, stand at a corner of the house when the sun is low and sight along the face of each gutter. Dents show up as dimples and shadows. Dented downspouts are easy to miss and easy to photograph.

If insurance is involved, check your policy and talk to your insurer. Our post on the first steps after a hail storm has the other checks worth making after hail.

What order should the roof and the gutters go in?

Roof first, gutters last. If the gutters are being replaced, the old ones come down before or during the tear-off. The crew strips the roof, swaps out any rotten decking and repairs or replaces damaged fascia. The drip edge, underlayment and shingles go on next. The new gutters go up after that, tucked under the finished drip edge and hung with a steady fall toward each downspout.

That order keeps falling debris off brand-new gutters. It also means the gutters are hung to fit the new roof edge.

Keeping your old gutters changes only the first step. They come down or get protected, the roof goes on, and then they are rehung or checked so the fall is still right. Ask the crew to run a hose into the high end of each run before they leave. It shows straight away whether the water reaches the downspout.

Is it cheaper to do the gutters and the roof at the same time?

It can be, though the saving is mostly in time and hassle rather than in the price of the gutters. One company plans both jobs and cleans up after both. Nobody has to come back months later to work along the edge of your new roof.

The bigger gain is at the joint. The roof edge and the gutter work as one piece, and they fit best when one plan covers both. When two companies work on that edge at different times, the second has to fit its work to the first, and if water later gets behind the gutter, it is hard to say whose work let it in.

New gutters are not automatically part of a re-roof, though. Paying to replace sound gutters buys you very little. Ask for them to be priced as a separate line, so you can see the cost and decide for yourself.

What should you ask your roofer about the gutters?

Ask these before you sign, and get the answers in writing.

  • Will you take my gutters down for the tear-off or work around them?
  • If you keep my gutters and they get bent, who pays to fix them?
  • Will the new drip edge hang into the gutter, not behind it?
  • If the fascia is rotten, will you replace it before the gutters go back up?
  • Will you check the fall to every downspout once the gutters are back?
  • Are the downspouts, elbows and extensions included, or only the gutters?

What can you check yourself before the inspection?

Watch your gutters in the next hard rain. From a window, note any spot where water sheets over the front or pours from a joint. Afterward, look for water still standing in the gutters and for stains on the fascia and the soffit below. Photograph any dents and loose runs from the ground.

If your neighborhood has an HOA, check its rules on roof and gutter colors before you choose anything. Then bring your notes and photos to the inspection. That way the gutters are settled before work starts, and nobody has to change the plan halfway through the job.

Understanding the Difference Between Roof Repair and Roof Replacement

Before deciding which option is best, it’s important to understand the difference between the two services.

Roof Repair

Roof repair focuses on fixing specific damaged areas without replacing the entire roofing system.

Common repairs include:

  • Replacing missing shingles
  • Fixing leaks
  • Repairing flashing
  • Sealing roof penetrations
  • Addressing minor storm damage

Repairs are usually recommended when the damage is isolated and the roof still has significant remaining lifespan.

Roof Replacement

Roof replacement involves removing the existing roofing system and installing a completely new roof.

This process may include:

  • Removing old shingles
  • Replacing damaged decking
  • Installing new underlayment
  • Adding new flashing and ventilation
  • Installing new roofing materials

Replacement is often necessary when the roof has widespread damage, advanced aging, or repeated repair issues.

When Roof Repair Is the Better Option

In many cases, repairing a roof is the most practical and cost-effective solution.

Minor or Isolated Damage

If the damage is limited to one area, repairs may be enough to restore the roof’s performance.

Examples include:

  • A few missing shingles
  • Small leaks
  • Minor flashing damage
  • Localized storm damage

Addressing small problems early can prevent larger repairs later.

Best Roofing Materials for Texas Heat, Wind, and Storms

Texas weather can be brutal on a roof. From scorching summer temperatures and intense UV exposure to hailstorms, heavy rain, and powerful winds, homeowners need roofing materials that can handle extreme conditions year-round. Choosing the right roofing system is one of the most important decisions you can make for protecting your home, improving energy efficiency, and reducing long-term maintenance costs.

Not all roofing materials perform the same in Texas climates. Some materials hold up better against heat, while others provide stronger protection against hail or wind damage. Understanding the strengths and weaknesses of each option can help homeowners make a smart long-term investment.

At F.I.T. Roofing & Construction, we help Texas homeowners select roofing systems built for durability, performance, and long-lasting protection. Here’s a complete guide to the best roofing materials for Texas heat, wind, and storms.

Why Texas Weather Is Tough on Roofs

Texas experiences some of the harshest weather conditions in the country. Roofing systems are constantly exposed to environmental stress that can shorten lifespan and increase repair needs.

Common weather challenges include:

  • Extreme summer heat
  • Intense UV radiation
  • Hailstorms
  • Heavy rain
  • Strong winds
  • Sudden temperature shifts
  • Humidity and moisture

These conditions can cause roofing materials to:

  • Crack or curl
  • Lose protective coatings
  • Fade over time
  • Develop leaks
  • Deteriorate prematurely

Choosing weather-resistant roofing materials is essential for long-term performance in Texas.

Asphalt Shingles

Asphalt shingles are the most popular roofing material in Texas due to their affordability, versatility, and solid overall performance.

Benefits of Asphalt Shingles

Homeowners choose asphalt shingles because they offer:

  • Affordable installation costs
  • Wide variety of colors and styles
  • Reliable weather protection
  • Easy repairs and maintenance
  • Good availability

Modern architectural shingles are stronger and more durable than older three-tab shingles, making them better suited for Texas weather.

Considerations for Texas Homes

While asphalt shingles perform well overall, prolonged heat exposure can shorten their lifespan if ventilation is poor. Extreme UV exposure may also cause shingles to fade or become brittle over time.

Impact-resistant asphalt shingles are highly recommended in hail-prone Texas regions because they provide better protection against storm damage.

Metal Roofing

Metal roofing has become increasingly popular throughout Texas because of its exceptional durability and energy efficiency.

Benefits of Metal Roofing

Metal roofs offer several advantages:

  • Long lifespan of 40–70 years
  • Excellent heat reflection
  • Superior wind resistance
  • Low maintenance requirements
  • Strong storm durability

Metal roofs reflect sunlight rather than absorbing heat, helping reduce attic temperatures and cooling costs during Texas summers.

Storm Protection

Metal roofing performs extremely well during:

  • High winds
  • Heavy rain
  • Severe storms
  • Fire exposure

Many metal roofing systems are also highly resistant to hail impacts compared to traditional shingles.

Things to Consider

Metal roofing generally costs more upfront than asphalt shingles, but the long lifespan and lower maintenance needs can provide long-term value.

Clay and Concrete Tile Roofing

Tile roofing is common in many Texas neighborhoods due to its durability, appearance, and heat resistance.

Advantages of Tile Roofing

Clay and concrete tiles offer:

  • Excellent heat resistance
  • Long lifespan
  • Strong curb appeal
  • Energy efficiency
  • Resistance to UV damage

Tile roofing naturally allows airflow beneath the material, helping regulate attic temperatures.

Durability in Texas Weather

Tile roofs perform well under intense heat and sun exposure. They are also highly resistant to fire and insect damage.

However, large hailstones may crack individual tiles during severe storms. Proper installation is critical for preventing storm-related issues.

Weight Considerations

Tile roofing is significantly heavier than asphalt shingles, so the home’s structure must be able to support the additional weight.

What to Do After Hail Damage to Your Roof in Texas

YHailstorms are one of the most common and destructive weather events in Texas. Every year, homeowners across the state deal with damaged shingles, roof leaks, dented gutters, and costly repairs caused by severe hailstorms. While some hail damage is obvious, many roofing problems remain hidden until water starts leaking into the home weeks or even months later.

Knowing what to do immediately after a hailstorm can help protect your home, reduce repair costs, and improve the chances of a successful insurance claim. Acting quickly is especially important because untreated hail damage can weaken your roof over time and lead to much larger structural problems.

At F.I.T. Roofing & Construction, we help Texas homeowners inspect, repair, and restore hail-damaged roofs safely and professionally. Here’s a complete guide on what to do after hail damages your roof in Texas.

Stay Safe and Assess the Situation

After a hailstorm passes, safety should always come first. Large hail, strong winds, lightning, and falling debris can create dangerous conditions around your property.

Before inspecting anything:

  • Wait until the storm has fully passed
  • Avoid climbing onto the roof yourself
  • Stay away from downed power lines
  • Watch for slippery surfaces or debris

Instead of immediately getting on the roof, start by inspecting your property safely from the ground.

Look for Visible Signs of Hail Damage

Not all hail damage is immediately noticeable, but there are several warning signs homeowners can look for after a storm.

Common Exterior Signs

Check for:

  • Dented gutters or downspouts
  • Damage to siding or windows
  • Broken outdoor fixtures
  • Fallen tree branches
  • Damaged vents or flashing

If these areas show impact damage, your roof may also have been affected.

Signs on Asphalt Shingles

Hail can damage shingles by knocking away protective granules or creating bruised areas that weaken the material.

Possible signs include:

  • Dark spots on shingles
  • Missing granules
  • Cracked shingles
  • Soft impact marks
  • Exposed roofing layers

Even small hail impacts can shorten your roof’s lifespan significantly over time.

Check Inside Your Home for Leaks

Hail damage often creates openings that allow water to enter the home during future rainstorms. Some leaks may not appear immediately, so it’s important to inspect the interior carefully.

Look for:

  • Water stains on ceilings
  • Peeling paint
  • Damp attic insulation
  • Mold or mildew smells
  • Dripping water

Pay close attention to the attic if accessible, since leaks often begin there before reaching living spaces.

Document All Damage

Proper documentation is extremely important if you plan to file an insurance claim.

Take clear photos of:

  • Roof damage visible from the ground
  • Dented gutters and flashing
  • Broken windows or siding
  • Water stains indoors
  • Storm debris around the property

Try to document the date and approximate time of the storm as well. Keeping organized records can help support your insurance claim later.

Schedule a Professional Roof Inspection

One of the biggest mistakes homeowners make after a hailstorm is assuming the roof is fine because there are no visible leaks. In reality, hail damage is often hidden and difficult to detect without professional experience.

A professional roofing inspection can identify:

  • Hidden shingle bruising
  • Compromised underlayment
  • Flashing damage
  • Structural concerns
  • Areas vulnerable to future leaks

At F.I.T. Roofing & Construction, we provide thorough roof inspections designed to identify both visible and hidden storm damage.

Avoid Delaying Repairs

Even minor hail damage can worsen over time if repairs are delayed. Once shingles lose their protective granules or develop cracks, Texas heat and rain can accelerate roof deterioration.

Untreated hail damage may lead to:

  • Roof leaks
  • Mold growth
  • Rotting decking
  • Interior water damage
  • Reduced energy efficiency

The sooner damage is addressed, the lower the risk of expensive long-term repairs.

  • Roof repairs
  • Full roof replacement

  • Storm-related structural repairs

Coverage typically depends on the severity of damage and the condition of the roof before the storm.
